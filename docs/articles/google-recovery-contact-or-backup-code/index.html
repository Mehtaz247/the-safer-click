Google Recovery Contacts and Backup Codes Do Different Jobs

Set up a recovery contact for account-recovery help, and keep backup codes for a missing 2-Step Verification method.

Published November 20, 2026Updated November 20, 20265 min read

The short version

  • A recovery contact can help when you forget your password or cannot sign in for another reason; it is not a set of sign-in codes.
  • Backup codes are one-time 2-Step Verification codes for when you cannot receive your normal second step.
  • Choose a trusted recovery contact and store backup codes somewhere private and separate from the phone or account they may need to help replace.

Next evidence review: March 20, 2027

Choose based on the problem you are preparing for

Set up a recovery contact if you want a trusted person available to help with Google Account recovery after you forget your password or cannot sign in for another reason. Google says a recovery contact does not receive your security alerts or notifications. This option is part of account recovery, not a replacement for your password or normal sign-in methods.

Generate backup codes if you use 2-Step Verification and need a fallback for its second step. Google says they can be used when you lose your phone, change your phone number, or otherwise cannot get codes by text, call, or Google Authenticator. A backup code does not replace a recovery contact: it is an 8-digit code you enter while signing in.

Prepare a recovery contact before you need one

Choose someone you know well and trust, such as a close friend or family member. Adding them shares your name, email address, and profile photo with that person so that they can decide whether to accept the request. Ask first, and make sure you have a way to contact them if you lose access to your Google Account.

On a computer, open your Google Account, choose Security & sign-in, then Recovery contacts. Select Add recovery contact, enter the person's email address, and send the request. Google says the request lasts seven days. After the person accepts, there is another seven-day period before the contact can be used for account recovery.

Not every recovery option is available for every region, account, or device. Google also says that work, school, and other group-managed accounts may not support these steps; ask that account's administrator if the option is unavailable.

  1. Ask a trusted person whether they are willing to be your recovery contact.
  2. In your Google Account, open Security & sign-in and then Recovery contacts.
  3. Send the request and check that the person accepts it before relying on the option.
  4. Review the contact later if your relationship or their email address changes.

Generate backup codes only if 2-Step Verification is on

Google requires 2-Step Verification to use backup codes. In your Google Account, open Security & sign-in, select 2-Step Verification, and find Backup codes. From there, Google offers options to get, download, print, refresh, or delete codes.

Google creates a set of 10 backup codes. Each code works once, then becomes inactive. Creating a new set automatically makes the old set inactive, so replace any printed or saved old list when you refresh the codes.

Treat backup codes like passwords. Do not share them. Google says it does not ask for a backup code except at sign-in. If you think someone else obtained your codes, create a new set so the old one becomes inactive.

  1. Confirm that 2-Step Verification is enabled on the Google Account.
  2. Open Google Account, Security & sign-in, 2-Step Verification, then Backup codes.
  3. Create a set and store it in a private, dependable place, such as with important documents.
  4. Mark a code as used after using it, and refresh the set if it is lost, exhausted, or exposed.

Store the two options differently

A recovery contact is a person, so the useful preparation is agreement and accurate contact details. Do not assume they can immediately restore access: Google controls the recovery process, and the contact becomes usable only after the documented waiting periods.

Backup codes are secrets. Keep them out of places that would be unavailable with your usual second factor, such as the same lost phone. A printed copy kept with important documents may be practical for some people. If you download a copy to a computer, remember that anyone who can access the file may be able to use an unused code.

Google says people enrolled in the Advanced Protection Program cannot download backup codes. If that applies to you, use the sign-in and recovery choices available in your account rather than assuming this download option will appear.

If you are already locked out

You cannot add recovery information while you are locked out or already in the account-recovery process. If you still have access, prepare recovery options now rather than waiting for a problem.

If you cannot sign in, Google directs people to its account recovery page and asks them to answer the questions as well as they can. A recovery contact or an unused backup code may be relevant depending on the sign-in screen and the options you prepared, but neither is a promise that access will be restored.

If a message, caller, or website asks you to disclose a backup code, stop and return to the Google Account sign-in flow directly. Backup codes should be entered only as part of your own sign-in.

Keep this boundary

Recovery contacts and backup codes solve different problems, and both must be set up while you can still access the account. Keep normal account details, passwords, and 2-Step Verification methods current as well.
