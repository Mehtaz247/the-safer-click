A Browser Warning Says to Call Support. Do Not Call Its Number.

A frightening page can imitate a system alert. Keep the warning from choosing your helper, your software, or your next payment.

Published October 23, 2026Updated October 23, 20263 min read

The short version

  • Do not call the number, install its suggested tool, or give the page's helper remote access.
  • Use independently chosen support if you suspect a real device problem.
  • If you already shared credentials, paid, or allowed remote access, respond to that exposure rather than just dismissing the warning.

Next evidence review: February 20, 2027

A loud warning is not proof of a diagnosis

Microsoft documents scam pages that imitate system errors, switch to full-screen display, repeat pop-ups, or play audio to make a browser appear locked. It says Microsoft error and warning messages do not contain support phone numbers. That is a Microsoft-specific statement, not a test for every possible device alert.

The FTC warns that fake support messages can impersonate familiar companies and steer people to a phone number. A logo, urgent countdown, or convincing voice is not a reason to let an unexpected warning choose whom you trust. Our default is to stop following the page's instructions.

Do not turn the page into a phone conversation

Do not call its number or accept a proposed remote-support session. The FTC describes callers who pretend to scan a computer, invent a problem, and charge to fix it. It also warns that support scams can appear in search results and advertisements. A new search is not, by itself, independent verification.

Our practical rule: leave the suspicious page and open a support route you already know, such as the manufacturer's official app or saved support site. Do not click a claimed cleanup button to prove the warning wrong. If you cannot leave normally, seek trusted help with closing the browser; do not accept the page's demand for payment as an exit method.

  1. Stop following the warning's instructions.
  2. Record whether you called, installed anything, shared information, or paid.
  3. Choose any needed helper independently and explain that exposure.

Separate a warning from access you actually gave

Write down whether you only saw the page, called someone, installed software, shared a password, or paid. That short record is more useful to a genuine helper than guessing what the warning means. Our recommendation is to disclose what happened clearly, without sending passwords or verification codes.

For Windows users who gave a scammer access, Microsoft recommends removing applications the scammer requested, running a full Windows Security scan, applying security updates, and changing passwords. It says a reset may be worth considering in some situations. A reset is not our automatic first step: use the official recovery instructions and understand the effect on your files and applications before proceeding.

If information or money was involved

The FTC advises changing a password given to a support scammer, including on other accounts where it was reused. Contact the payment provider through a known route if you paid; Microsoft specifically advises cardholders to contest scam charges with their card provider. Recovery is not guaranteed.

For U.S. readers, report the scam at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Elsewhere, use your national reporting service. On a work or school device, our recommendation is to contact your organization's established IT channel promptly rather than attempting major recovery changes yourself.
