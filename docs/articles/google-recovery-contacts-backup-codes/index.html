Google Recovery Contacts and Backup Codes Do Different Jobs

Set up a trusted recovery contact for account-recovery help, and keep backup codes private for a lost 2-Step Verification method.

Published November 23, 2026Updated November 23, 20266 min read

The short version

  • A recovery contact may help you get back into a Google Account when you forget a password or cannot sign in for another reason.
  • Backup codes are one-time 2-Step Verification codes for when you cannot use your normal second step, such as after losing a phone.
  • Choose a recovery contact you know and trust, and keep backup codes private in a safe place.

Next evidence review: March 23, 2027

Prepare both, but do not treat them as the same tool

Use a recovery contact as part of Google Account recovery planning. Google says a recovery contact can help if you forget your password or cannot sign in for another reason. Use backup codes as a fallback for the second step of sign-in when 2-Step Verification is on and you cannot receive or generate your usual code.

The practical decision is simple. Add a recovery contact while you still have access to the account, then create backup codes if you use 2-Step Verification. Neither option is a promise that every future sign-in problem will be resolved, but they address different points of failure.

What a recovery contact is for

Google says recovery information can help you regain access if you forget your password, someone else uses your account, or you are locked out for another reason. A recovery contact is one recovery option. Google recommends choosing someone you know well and trust, such as a close friend or family member.

A recovery contact does not receive your Google security alerts or notifications. When you send the request, however, Google says the person receives your name, email address, and profile photo. Discuss the request with them first so they can recognize and accept it.

The invitation is not ready for recovery immediately. Google says the request expires after seven days if it is not accepted. After acceptance, there is also a seven-day period before the contact can be used for account recovery.

  1. Choose a person you know well and trust.
  2. Open your Google Account and select Security & sign-in.
  3. Under “How you sign in to Google,” select Recovery contacts, then Add recovery contact.
  4. Enter the person's email address and send the request.
  5. Check later that the contact accepted it and remember that Google documents a seven-day wait before recovery use.

What backup codes are for

Backup codes are for a different situation: you are signing in with 2-Step Verification but cannot use the normal second step. Google gives examples including losing your phone, changing your phone number, or being unable to receive codes by text, call, or Google Authenticator.

Each backup code works once. Google provides a set of 10 codes, and a code becomes inactive after you use it to sign in. Creating a new set also makes the previous set inactive. That makes it useful to replace a set if you run out of codes, lose them, or think someone else obtained them.

Treat backup codes like a sign-in secret. Google says not to share them and says it will not ask for a backup code except at sign-in. A caller, message, or unexpected form asking for one is not a reason to provide it.

Create and store backup codes before you need one

Backup codes require 2-Step Verification to be on. While you can sign in normally, open your Google Account, select Security & sign-in, then 2-Step Verification. In the Backup codes area, select Continue and choose Get backup codes.

Google offers options to download or print the codes. Store them somewhere private and reliable, separate from a phone that might be lost. Google suggests a safe place such as where you keep a passport or other important documents. Do not put the codes in a shared note, send them in a message, or give them to a recovery contact.

If you need one during sign-in, choose Try another way and then Enter one of your 8-digit backup codes. Mark the code as used afterward so you do not try it again.

  1. Confirm that 2-Step Verification is turned on.
  2. Go to Google Account, Security & sign-in, 2-Step Verification, then Backup codes.
  3. Select Get backup codes and save or print the set in a private location.
  4. If a code is used, lost, or suspected to be exposed, create a new set so the old set becomes inactive.

Check availability and act before a lockout

Google says not every recovery option is available for every region, account, or device. Work, school, and other group-managed accounts can have different settings, so ask the administrator if the documented steps do not apply. Google also says people in its Advanced Protection Program cannot download backup codes.

Do this setup while you can still access the account. Google says you cannot add recovery information while you are locked out or already in the account-recovery process. If you cannot sign in, use Google's account recovery page and answer its questions as accurately as you can.

Review these options after a major change such as replacing a phone number, changing the email account you use regularly, or ending a close relationship with a recovery contact. The aim is not to collect every option; it is to keep the options you rely on current and under your control.

Keep this boundary

A recovery contact can support account recovery, while a backup code replaces a normal 2-Step Verification method for one sign-in. Keep the contact relationship current and keep every unused backup code private.
