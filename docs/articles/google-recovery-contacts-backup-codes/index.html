Google Recovery Contacts and Backup Codes Do Different Jobs

Set up a recovery contact for account recovery and keep backup codes for a missing second-step method. Neither replaces the other.

Published November 21, 2026Updated November 21, 20265 min read

The short version

  • A recovery contact can help when you forget your password or cannot sign in for another reason; it is an account-recovery option, not a code you keep yourself.
  • Backup codes are one-time 2-Step Verification codes for when you cannot receive your normal second-step code.
  • Prepare both while you can sign in, and keep backup codes private and separate from the device you rely on for sign-in.

Choose the option that matches the problem

Use a recovery contact to add another route for getting back into a Google Account if you forget your password or cannot sign in for another reason. Google says to choose someone you know well and trust, such as a close friend or family member. The contact receives a request containing your name, email address, and profile photo, so ask before sending it.

Use backup codes when 2-Step Verification is already on but your usual second step is unavailable. Google gives examples including losing a phone, changing a phone number, or being unable to receive codes by text, call, or Google Authenticator. A backup code is entered during sign-in as the second step; it is not a substitute for knowing the account password.

These options can complement each other. A recovery contact is intended to help with account recovery, while a backup code is a prepared fallback for the second step of an otherwise normal sign-in. Neither option promises that every access problem will be resolved.

Set up a recovery contact before you need one

While signed in, open your Google Account, choose Security & sign-in, and look under “How you sign in to Google” for Recovery contacts. Select Add recovery contact, enter the person's email address, and send the request. The person must accept it.

Do not treat the invitation as instantly available. Google says the request expires after seven days if it is not accepted. After a contact accepts, there is another seven-day period before that contact can be used for account recovery. Confirm the arrangement early rather than waiting until you are locked out.

This setting may not appear for every account, region, or device. Google also says that accounts managed through work, school, or another group may use different controls, in which case the administrator is the appropriate source of help. If Recovery contacts is not available, do not try to add it during an account-recovery attempt: Google says recovery information cannot be added while you are locked out or already in the recovery process.

  1. Choose a person you know well and trust, and ask whether they are willing to be your recovery contact.
  2. In your signed-in Google Account, go to Security & sign-in, then Recovery contacts.
  3. Send the request and make sure it is accepted within seven days.
  4. Allow the seven-day post-acceptance period before relying on the contact for recovery.

Create backup codes only if you use 2-Step Verification

Backup codes require 2-Step Verification to be turned on. In your signed-in Google Account, go to Security & sign-in, open 2-Step Verification, then find Backup codes and choose Continue. Google lets you get codes, download them, or print them from that area.

Google creates a set of 10 backup codes. Each code is eight digits and works once; after you use one to sign in, it becomes inactive. If you create a new set, the old set automatically becomes inactive. Refresh the set if you lose the codes, run out of them, or think someone else obtained them.

Keep the codes as carefully as you would a password. Store a printed copy with important documents or use another private storage method that you can reach if your usual phone is unavailable. Do not send a code to someone claiming to be support. Google says it never asks for a backup code except during sign-in.

  1. Confirm that 2-Step Verification is on for the account.
  2. Open Google Account, Security & sign-in, 2-Step Verification, then Backup codes.
  3. Create a set and store it privately somewhere available when your normal phone is not.
  4. When using one, select Try another way during sign-in and enter an unused eight-digit backup code.
  5. Replace the set promptly if it is lost, exhausted, or may have been exposed.

Add other recovery information and review changes carefully

Google also offers recovery phone numbers and recovery email addresses. A recovery phone can be used to send a code if you are locked out and to alert you to suspicious activity. Google recommends using a phone that receives text messages, belongs only to you, is used regularly, and stays with you. It specifically advises against using a Google Voice number as a recovery phone because that can leave you unable to receive the needed code.

For a recovery email, Google advises choosing an address you use regularly but not the address used to sign in to the Google Account. Review recovery options while you still have access, and remove a recovery contact only when you no longer want that person associated with the account.

Changes to recovery information can have delays. Google says a changed recovery phone number can take up to seven days to take effect, and may send codes to previous information for seven days after a change to recovery information or another authentication factor. If an unexpected change or sign-in occurs, use Google's account-recovery process and secure the account rather than assuming a backup code or contact alone will fix the situation.

Keep this boundary

Do not share backup codes with a recovery contact. They serve different purposes: a contact is someone Google may use in account recovery, while a backup code is a private, one-time second-step credential.

A short preparation checklist

The best time to prepare recovery options is while you can sign in normally. Take a few minutes to check which options your account offers, make sure the people and contact details are current, and make a plan for where private backup codes will be stored.

Revisit the setup after changing a phone number, losing a device, changing a trusted relationship, or refreshing backup codes. The aim is not to create as many recovery routes as possible. It is to keep a small set of recovery information that you understand, control, and can still reach when your ordinary sign-in method is unavailable.

  1. Check whether Recovery contacts is available on your account and invite a trusted person if it is.
  2. Turn on 2-Step Verification if appropriate for your account, then generate backup codes.
  3. Store the codes privately and do not keep the only copy on the phone you may lose.
  4. Review recovery phone and email details, including whether you still control them.
  5. Refresh backup codes and update recovery details when circumstances change.
